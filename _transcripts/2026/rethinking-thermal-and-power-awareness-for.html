So let me start with the motivation. The power density of AI racks is increasing rapidly as modern GPU consume more and more power. When cooling is insufficient, GPU temperature rises, and thermal throttling can reduce the GPU operating frequency and degrade performance.

0:54 Liquid-cooling provides much higher cooling capacity than conventional air cooling, and is therefore becoming increasingly important for high-density AI systems. However, liquid-cooling does not necessarily guarantee uniform cooling across all GPUs. Cooling conditions may still vary depending on GPU placement, coolant flow path, and workloads on neighboring GPUs and servers. And prior work has also suggested that coolant flow distribution and cooling performance may vary with rack position.

1:32 And existing LLM serving systems primarily make scheduling and routing decisions based on IT-side metrics, such as server load, latency, throughput, and GPU utilization, and so on. However, the available thermal and power headroom may differ across GPUs because of their physical placement and cooling conditions. At the same time, incoming requests also have different characteristics, such as input lengths, output lengths, and KV cache reuse.

2:03 These infrastructure conditions and workload characteristics are not usually considered together in current LLM serving systems. Therefore, our research question is, how should LLM serving jointly account for data center infrastructure conditions and AI workload characteristics? To explore this question, we conducted measurements in a production-scale liquid-cooled GPU rack.

2:40 To answer this research question, our work has three main parts. First, we characterize the thermal and cooling behavior of a production-liquid-cooled H200 GPU rack. We examine cooling heterogeneity across GPUs, the conditions that lead to power capping or thermal throttling, and the behavior of the CDU under different workload conditions. Second, we characterize the performance and energy efficiency of LLM serving. We analyze GPU thermal and power behavior together with LLM request characteristics, such as token lengths, concurrency, and KV cache reuse. And third, based on these measurements, we derive cross-layer implications for future LLM serving systems. In particular, we discuss how serving systems should jointly consider AI workload characteristics and data center infrastructure conditions. I will first present our measurement environment and cooling side results, and then move to the LLM serving results and system implications.

12:35 And our next observation concerns the request-level trade-offs between energy efficiency and latency. The left figure shows the effect of input length. The benefit was especially large for requests with long inputs because cache reuse avoids more prefill computation. These results highlight an important distinction. Input length is determined by the request and cannot be directly controlled by the serving system. In contrast, concurrency and request routing are controllable. Therefore, serving systems should maintain an energy-efficient concurrency range and use cache aware routing and scheduling to maximize KV cache reuse. Based on these observations, we derive three implications for cross-layer control of liquid-cooled LLM servings. The main message is that efficient LLM serving requires joint awareness of both AI workload characteristics and physical infrastructure conditions. First, request routing and workload placement should be thermal aware even in liquid-cooled rack GPUs. Our results show that even GPUs in the liquid-cooled rack are not thermally interchangeable. Their temperatures and available thermal headroom can differ depending on GPU position and surrounding workloads. Therefore, routing decisions should consider the thermal condition of each GPU.

15:21 And second, serving control should be request aware. As shown on the previous slide, concurrency and KV cache reuse can substantially affect both performance and energy efficiency. In some cases, by more than an order of magnitude. Therefore, optimizing these serving level parameters is, of course, also essential. And third, cooling control should be workload-adaptive. Rather than operating independently of the AI workload, the CDU and facility cooling systems should respond to aggregate GPU load and thermal demand.

16:07 From the perspective of reducing cooling energy and associated operational carbon emissions, intelligent control of the CDU, including coolant flow rate, and pump operation may also be important. Together, these results motivate a cross-layer controller that coordinates request routing, GPU operation and cooling infrastructure.
