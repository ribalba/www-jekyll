0:43 Well, cloud users are massive organizations in their own right, and they deploy applications spanning thousands of microservices on various different cloud platforms. And for carbon awareness to really work for these applications, it needs to percolate throughout the entire hardware systems, hypervisor, and the software stacks. Clearly, this is a super challenging problem. One approach that the systems community has considered in the past is to expose carbon APIs to application developers so that they can understand the carbon intensity of their application and rewrite them to be carbon aware. While this is a super powerful, powerful approach, it's limited because it puts a lot of burden on application developers. And unfortunately, developer time is already a business bottleneck for a lot of cloud users. Cloud users want to keep churning out new features and functionalities to attract and retain their own customer bases. So it's very unlikely that developers are going to have a lot of time to iterate on carbon optimizations. Even if a developer wanted to write carbon aware code, there is minimal training and tooling available to them to do so. And finally, rewriting applications in the case of these large-scale microservice deployments might not even be feasible because they often rely on legacy or black box application code, which cannot easily be modified, be modified, or it's used as libraries which developers don't regularly interact with. The first, who within these large-scale cloud user organizations can make an impactful and a timely difference? And second, how can we, a systems researcher, help these people? So to answer these questions, let's take a closer look at large-scale application teams at one of such cloud users. It turns out almost every large-scale application team has an operations team. And the role of these operators is to collaborate with developers to deploy their applications on cloud infrastructure. In addition, operators also monitor these deployments to ensure that they're meeting the service-level objectives. For example, they might choose to trade-off performance for reduction in cost or vice versa. Operators might do not only interact with members of their own organizations, but they also interact very closely with provider teams. This kind of a relationship works out very great because providers have a lot more visibility into their own infrastructure and services, whereas operators might know when service faults are happening and they can communicate those to providers. Given that operators are this narrow waist between cloud users and provider organizations, we believe that they are best suited to introduce carbon awareness into the cloud. Specifically, we propose viewing carbon as a operations concern by including it as a metric that they trade-off alongside the other metrics that they already trade-off, like cost, latency, and reliability. We believe that this is likely to be an easier problem for operators to handle than rewriting applications, at least in the near term. Empowered with visibility into carbon metrics, operators can then help developers and managers within their own organizations to become more carbon aware. For example, operators could work with developers to pinpoint which applications are carbon hotspots so that the precious few cycles that developers have could be spent on optimizing those services. Similarly, operators can also work with managers when making planning decisions to choose carbon efficient cloud platforms or services so that their future projects end up becoming more and more carbon efficient. Operators can also directly communicate with provider teams and this communication can happen in both ways. This is really necessary because

So in the operator to provider direction, we propose that application communication, we propose that providers should introduce the notion of eco modes for cloud applications. You might have come across the term eco modes in everyday scenarios like cars or washing machines, and our idea is really to capture this notion of coarse-grained carbon trade-offs in the context of cloud applications. Eco modes are super necessary because providers don't have a lot of visibility into application information, and even though they might have some super cool carbon optimization available to them, they have to assume the worst-case SLOs for a lot of the applications, and they cannot apply them.

6:14 Enabling the eco mode can signal to the provider that, hey, we are okay with certain kinds of trade-offs being made, and that unlocks carbon optimizations to be implemented for operator services. And there doesn't need to be just one kind of eco mode. There can be different kinds of eco modes that capture different kinds of trade-offs, and to give you a couple of examples from today, we could have carbon-aware scheduling that is triggered if an application is signaled to be delay-tolerant. Eco modes can also help reduce embodied emissions if the operator signals to the provider that they're okay with low reliability mode operation for certain kinds of requests or certain kinds of end users. Eco modes actually get even cooler than that because developers can now start implementing specific trade-offs that are triggered only when the eco mode is enabled.

7:07 As a concrete example, let's say a service was running machine learning inference and was now switched on to eco mode. The developer can then choose to switch a model that performs the same task but has different carbon and accuracy trade-offs. In the other direction, we propose that providers expose coarse-grained service level carbon metrics to operators, and this is important because it helps guide optimization efforts and trade-off decisions for operators. One thing we want to emphasize here is that approximate metrics are good enough to expose for now because a lot of the code so far has been written without carbon awareness or carbon efficiency in mind. So there's likely a lot of low-hanging fruit which can be optimized and it could help reduce carbon emissions. Eventually, as carbon trade-offs become more and more nuanced, providers can start exposing fine-grained metrics, some of which you've heard about in the previous talks. We want to emphasize here also that the way the metrics are exposed should be integrated into existing operator workflows. For example, operators today rely on dashboards which help them manage the complexity of monitoring and resolving issues in tons of microservices that they're deploying.

8:29 Integrating a notion of carbon metrics along with some thresholding mechanisms like carbon budgets can help operators treat this as a resource management problem that they can use their existing workflow to optimize.
